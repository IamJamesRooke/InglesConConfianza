# Comparative and Superlative Rules

Use a comparative to compare two things and a superlative to identify the highest or lowest member of a group.

| Form | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| comparative | adjective + **-er** or **more + adjective** | harder / more important |
| superlative | **the** + adjective + **-est** or **the most + adjective** | the hardest / the most important |

## Short adjectives: **-er / -est**

Most one-syllable adjectives take **-er** and **-est**:

| Base | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| hard | harder | the hardest |
| fast | faster | the fastest |
| slow | slower | the slowest |
| cheap | cheaper | the cheapest |

If the adjective ends in **-e**, add **-r/-st**: **safe → safer → the safest**.

Some short words double the final consonant: **big → bigger → the biggest**.

## Adjectives ending in consonant + **y**

Change **y** to **i** before **-er/-est**:

| Base | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| funny | funnier | the funniest |
| happy | happier | the happiest |
| easy | easier | the easiest |

This spelling pattern matters more than a simple syllable count: **happy** and **funny** have two syllables but still take **-ier/-iest**.

## Longer adjectives: **more / most**

Most longer adjectives use **more** and **the most**:

| Base | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| important | more important | the most important |
| difficult | more difficult | the most difficult |
| beautiful | more beautiful | the most beautiful |

Do not combine the two patterns: **more difficult**, not *difficulter* or *more difficulter*.

## Core comparison phrases

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| más difícil | harder / more difficult |
| más fácil | easier |
| más importante | more important |
