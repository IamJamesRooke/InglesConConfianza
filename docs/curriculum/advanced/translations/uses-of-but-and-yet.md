# Uses of **But** and **Yet**

> **I haven't finished yet, yet I've finished everything but the ending.**

**But** and **yet** can both introduce a contrast. **But** can also mean **excepto**, while **yet** can mean **aún** or **todavía**. Their position and the surrounding grammar reveal the intended meaning.

## **But** Meaning However

Use **but** to connect two contrasting ideas.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Quería acompañarlos, pero no pude salir de la oficina a tiempo. | I wanted to join you, but I couldn't leave the office in time. |

The second idea conflicts with the speaker's intention. **Couldn't** is the standard contraction of **could not**.

## **But** Meaning Except

Before a noun or pronoun, **but** can mean **excepto**, **salvo** or **menos**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Todos entregaron el informe a tiempo menos Andrés, que lo envió al día siguiente. | Everyone but Andrés submitted the report on time; he sent his the next day. |

Here **but Andrés** excludes Andrés from **everyone**. It does not connect two contrasting clauses.

## **Not X but Y**

Use **not X but Y** to reject one description and replace it with the correct one. Spanish normally uses **no... sino...**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El problema no es el precio, sino el tiempo que tardará la entrega. | The problem isn't the price but the time the delivery will take. |

**But rather** can emphasize the correction: **The problem isn't the price but rather the delivery time.** The shorter **not X but Y** structure is usually sufficient.

## Spanish **Sino** Versus **Si No**

Spanish **sino** corrects or replaces an idea and becomes **but** or **but rather**. The two-word expression **si no** introduces a negative condition and becomes **if not**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| No llamaremos hoy, sino mañana. | We won't call today but tomorrow. |
| Si no llamamos hoy, perderemos la reserva. | If we don't call today, we'll lose the reservation. |

When it states the consequence of not acting, English **otherwise** can also translate **si no**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Tenemos que llamar hoy; si no, perderemos la reserva. | We have to call today; otherwise, we'll lose the reservation. |

## Sentence-Final **Though**

In informal English, **though** can appear at the end of a sentence and mean **sin embargo**, **eso sí** or **aunque**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El apartamento es pequeño. Sin embargo, tiene mucha luz natural. | The apartment is small. It has plenty of natural light, though. |

Sentence-final **though** creates a contrast with the previous statement. For a clear core sentence, use either **but** or final **though**; casual speakers sometimes combine them for extra emphasis.

## Contrasting **Yet**

As a connector, **yet** means **pero**, **sin embargo** or **aun así**. It often makes the contrast sound more surprising than **but** does.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El apartamento es pequeño, pero tiene suficiente espacio para una familia de cuatro personas. | The apartment is small, yet it has enough space for a family of four. |

Both **but** and **yet** are possible here, but **yet** emphasizes the unexpected amount of space.

## Temporal **Yet**

In questions and negative statements, **yet** commonly means **ya**, **aún** or **todavía** and usually appears at the end.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Ya terminaste el informe? —No, todavía no lo he terminado. | “Have you finished the report yet?” “No, I haven't finished it yet.” |

In the question, Spanish naturally uses **ya**. In the negative answer, the same English word becomes **todavía** or **aún**.

## **Not Yet**

Use **not yet** when something has not happened but is still expected.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El cliente todavía no ha firmado el contrato, pero esperamos recibirlo esta tarde. | The client hasn't signed the contract yet, but we expect to receive it this afternoon. |

The expected signature distinguishes **not yet** from a permanent **never**.

## **Have Yet to**

The more formal structure **have yet to + base verb** means that something has not happened up to now.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| La empresa aún no ha explicado cómo protegerá los datos de los usuarios. | The company has yet to explain how it will protect users' data. |

This means **the company hasn't explained it yet**, but the structure is more common in formal reporting.

## Minimal-Difference Teaching Sentence

> **I haven't finished yet, yet I've finished everything but the ending.**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Todavía no he terminado, pero ya terminé todo menos el final. | I haven't finished yet, yet I've finished everything but the ending. |

The first **yet** means **todavía**, the second means **pero**, and **but** means **menos** or **excepto**. The standard contractions **haven't** and **I've** keep the sentence natural while its repeated short sounds make the three meanings memorable.
