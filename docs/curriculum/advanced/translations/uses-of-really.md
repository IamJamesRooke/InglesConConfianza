# Uses of **Really**

> **Really? That really is a really realistic replica.**

**Really** can ask whether something is true, confirm that it is true, react with surprise, or intensify a description. Spanish expresses these roles with forms such as **de verdad**, **en serio**, **realmente**, **muy** and **mucho**.

## **Really** Meaning Truly

Use **really** to ask whether something is true or to emphasize that it is true.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿Laura de verdad renunció, o solo está pensando en irse? | Did Laura really quit, or is she only thinking about leaving? |

The question contrasts a real resignation with a possible plan, so **really** means **de verdad** or **realmente**.

## **Really?** as a Reaction

On its own, **Really?** can express surprise, doubt or interest. Its tone helps determine whether it sounds curious or skeptical.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —Conseguí el trabajo en Toronto. —¿En serio? ¡Felicitaciones! | “I got the job in Toronto.” “Really? Congratulations!” |

Here **Really?** means **¿En serio?** or **¿De verdad?**

## **Really** as an Intensifier

Before an adjective or adverb, **really** commonly means **muy** and expresses a strong degree.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Es muy importante verificar el número de cuenta porque no podremos cancelar la transferencia. | It's really important to verify the account number because we won't be able to cancel the transfer. |

The standard contractions **it's** and **won't** are natural in speech and informal writing. In this context, **really important** is stronger than **kind of important** and normally stronger than **pretty important**.

Depending on tone and context, Spanish may naturally use **muy importante**, **realmente importante** or occasionally **bastante importante**. Do not memorize **bastante** as the fixed translation of **really**.

## **Really + Verb**

Before a verb, **really** can intensify a feeling, desire, need or opinion. Spanish often uses **mucho**, not **muy**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Me gusta mucho este apartamento, pero no puedo pagar el arriendo. | I really like this apartment, but I can't afford the rent. |

Use **I really like it → me gusta mucho**, not the unnatural literal translation *me muy gusta*.

## **Not Really**

**Not really** gives a softened or qualified negative answer. It often means **no mucho**, **en realidad no** or **no exactamente**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Te gustó la película? —La verdad, no mucho; la historia era demasiado predecible. | “Did you like the movie?” “Not really; the story was too predictable.” |

The speaker answers no but makes the rejection less blunt.

## Minimal-Difference Teaching Sentence

> **Really? That really is a really realistic replica.**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿En serio? Esa sí es una réplica muy realista. | Really? That really is a really realistic replica. |

The opening **Really?** expresses surprise. The second **really** confirms that the statement is true, while the third intensifies **realistic**. The repeated **r** sounds turn the three grammatical roles into a short pronunciation drill.
