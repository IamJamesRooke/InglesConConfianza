# Uses of **One** and Related Forms

> **Only once, one lonely contestant ate alone after she'd won lunch for two.**

**One** can express the number one or replace a singular countable noun. The related-looking forms **ones**, **once**, **only** and **alone** perform different jobs, and not all of them share the same vowel sound.

## **One** Replacing a Singular Noun

Use **one** when the listener knows which singular countable noun you mean and repeating it would sound unnecessary.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Hay tres chaquetas sobre la mesa; quiero la azul, no la negra. | There are three jackets on the table; I want the blue one, not the black one. |

English needs **one** after **blue** and **black** because the adjectives replace the singular countable noun **jacket**.

## **Which One?** and **Which Ones?**

Use singular **which one?** to choose one item and plural **which ones?** to choose more than one.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Cuál quieres? —La pequeña. | “Which one do you want?” “The small one.” |
| —¿Cuáles quieres? —Las pequeñas. | “Which ones do you want?” “The small ones.” |

The plural marker moves to **ones**: **the small ones**, not *the smalls*.

## **This One** and **That One**

Use **this one** for a nearby singular item and **that one** for a more distant singular item.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Esta taza está rota, pero esa está en perfecto estado. | This cup is broken, but that one is in perfect condition. |

English can also say simply **this** and **that** when the item is clear. Adding **one** emphasizes the choice among similar objects.

## **These** and **Those**

For plural items, **these** and **those** can stand alone without **ones**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Estos zapatos me quedan bien, pero esos son demasiado grandes. | These shoes fit me, but those are too big. |

**These ones** and **those ones** occur in informal and regional English, especially when contrasting sets, but many speakers prefer the cleaner standard forms **these** and **those**.

## **The First, Next** and **Last One**

Add **one** or **ones** after words such as **first**, **next** and **last** when they replace countable nouns.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El primer ejercicio fue fácil, los dos siguientes fueron difíciles y el último fue imposible. | The first exercise was easy, the next two were difficult, and the last one was impossible. |

With an explicit number, English normally says **the next two**, not *the next two ones*. Without the noun or a number, use forms such as **the first one**, **the next one**, **the last one**, **the first ones** and **the last ones**.

## **Once** Meaning One Time

**Once** means **una vez**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Solo he visitado Cartagena una vez, pero quiero volver el próximo año. | I've only visited Cartagena once, but I want to go back next year. |

**I've** is the standard contraction of **I have**. In ordinary speech, prefer **once** to **one time** unless the number itself needs emphasis.

## **Only** Meaning No More Than

Before a quantity, **only** limits it and often means **solo**, **solamente** or **apenas**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Solo queda una entrada, así que no podemos asistir los dos. | There's only one ticket left, so we can't both attend. |

The position of **only** matters: **only one ticket** limits the number of tickets.

## **The Only One** and **The Only Thing**

Use **the only one** for the unique person or countable item and **the only thing** for one unique fact, object, action or concern.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Natalia fue la única que recordó la contraseña. | Natalia was the only one who remembered the password. |
| Lo único que necesito es acceso al documento. | The only thing I need is access to the document. |

Do not translate every Spanish **lo único** mechanically as **the only thing**; choose the noun that the context requires.

## **Alone** Versus **Only**

**Alone** means **solo**, without another person. **Only** limits a number or possibility.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Diana fue sola a la conferencia porque era la única de su equipo con una entrada. | Diana went to the conference alone because she was the only one on her team with a ticket. |

The first idea concerns company; the second concerns uniqueness.

## **Alone** Versus **Lonely**

**Alone** describes being without other people. **Lonely** describes the unhappy feeling that can result.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Andrés vive solo, pero no se siente solo porque visita a sus amigos todos los días. | Andrés lives alone, but he doesn't feel lonely because he visits his friends every day. |

A person can be **alone** without being **lonely**, or feel **lonely** while surrounded by people.

## Pronunciation: **One, Won, Once, Only** and **Alone**

- **One** and **won** are homophones: `/wʌn/`.
- **Once** begins with the same sound as **one** and ends in `/s/`: `/wʌns/`.
- **Only** begins with the long **o** sound `/oʊ/`: `/ˈoʊnli/`.
- **Alone** contains that same long **o** sound in its stressed syllable: `/əˈloʊn/`.

The spelling can tempt learners to pronounce all four alike, but **one/once** and **only/alone** form two different vowel groups.

## Minimal-Difference Pronunciation Sentence

> **Only once, one lonely contestant ate alone after she'd won lunch for two.**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Solo una vez, una concursante solitaria comió sola después de haberse ganado un almuerzo para dos. | Only once, one lonely contestant ate alone after she'd won lunch for two. |

This intentionally playful sentence contrasts **only**, **once**, **one**, **lonely**, **alone** and homophonous **won**. **She'd** is the standard contraction of **she had** in the past-perfect phrase **she'd won**.
