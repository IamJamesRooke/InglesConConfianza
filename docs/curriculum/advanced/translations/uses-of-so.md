# Uses of **So**

> **So, if sewing is so easy, why do so many people sew so much—and why do I think so too?**

**So** can express degree, quantity, result, purpose, agreement, a conclusion or a complete idea already understood from context. Its Spanish translation changes with each use.

## **So** Meaning **Tan**

Use **so** before a description to express a strong degree.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| La película fue tan divertida que la vimos dos veces. | The movie was so funny that we watched it twice. |

The common pattern **so...that** connects the strong degree with its result: it was **so funny that** they watched it again.

## **So Much** and **So Many**

Spanish provides the direct distinction:

- **tanto trabajo → so much work**
- **tantos correos → so many emails**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Tengo tanto trabajo y tantos correos pendientes que no podré salir temprano. | I've got so much work and so many unanswered emails that I won't be able to leave early. |

**I've** and **won't** are standard contractions. The separate intensifier contrast lesson compares **so much/many** with **too much/many**, **not that much/many** and **as much/many as**.

## **So** Meaning **Así Que**

Use **so** to introduce a result or consequence.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Había demasiado tráfico, así que llegamos veinte minutos tarde. | There was too much traffic, so we arrived twenty minutes late. |

The traffic caused the late arrival. This **so** connects two complete ideas.

## Sentence-Opening **So**

At the beginning of a sentence, **so** can introduce a conclusion, the next step or a new question. Spanish often uses **entonces**, **bueno** or no separate word.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Entonces, ya revisamos los costos. ¿Qué vamos a hacer ahora? | So, we've reviewed the costs. What are we going to do now? |

This conversational **so** organizes the discussion; it does not mean **tan**.

## Purpose: **So That**

Use **so that** for **para que** when an action is intended to produce a result.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Hablo despacio para que todos puedan entenderme. | I speak slowly so that everyone can understand me. |

In conversation, **that** is often omitted: **I speak slowly so everyone can understand me.**

Accented **¿para qué?** asks about purpose and is normally **what for?** or **why?**, not **so**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —Necesito otra llave. —¿Para qué? | “I need another key.” “What for?” |

## Agreement: **So Am I, So Do I, So Can I**

Use **so + helping verb + person** to agree with a positive statement.

| First statement | Agreement |
|---|---|
| **I'm tired.** | **So am I.** |
| **I work from home.** | **So do I.** |
| **I can drive.** | **So can I.** |
| **I'll attend.** | **So will I.** |
| **I've finished.** | **So have I.** |

Repeat the helping verb from the first statement. When the first statement has no visible helping verb, use **do**: **I work from home → So do I**.

Spanish **yo también** can also become **me too** in informal conversation. Compare:

- **So do I / Me too → Yo también.**
- **I also do it → También lo hago.**

The second sentence says that the speaker performs the same action; it is not the short agreement pattern.

For agreement with a negative statement, use **neither** instead: **I can't drive. Neither can I.**

## **I Think So**

After verbs such as **think**, **hope**, **suppose** and **guess**, **so** can replace a complete idea that is already understood.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Va a aprobar Laura el examen? —Creo que sí; estudió toda la semana. | “Will Laura pass the exam?” “I think so; she studied all week.” |

**I think so** means **creo que sí** or, literally as a teaching bridge, **creo que es así**. It is more natural than repeating **I think Laura will pass the exam**.

Use **I think it's like that** only when **like that** describes a manner, arrangement or process.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Creo que el sistema funciona así, pero revisemos el manual para estar seguros. | I think the system works like that, but let's check the manual to be sure. |

## **I Don't Think So**

Use **I don't think so** for **no lo creo** or **creo que no**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Está abierto el banco hoy? —No lo creo; hoy es festivo. | “Is the bank open today?” “I don't think so; today's a holiday.” |

**I don't think it's like that** refers more literally to the manner or situation being “like that.” It is not the normal replacement for **I don't think so**.

## **If So**

**If so** means **si es así**, **en ese caso** or **si la respuesta es sí**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿El cliente ya aprobó el diseño? Si es así, podemos publicarlo hoy. | Has the client approved the design? If so, we can publish it today. |

Here **so** replaces the complete idea **the client has approved the design**.

## **So?** and **So What?**

On its own, **So?** asks for the consequence or relevance of what was just said. **So what?** dismisses that information more strongly.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —El competidor tiene una oficina más grande. —¿Y qué? La nuestra atiende a más clientes. | “The competitor has a larger office.” “So what? Ours serves more clients.” |

Depending on tone, **So?** can mean **¿y?**, **¿entonces?** or **¿y eso qué?** Spanish *¿así qué?* is not the natural translation of **So what?**

## **So Far**

**So far** means **hasta ahora** when referring to progress up to the present.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Hasta ahora hemos migrado todas las lecciones previstas para esta semana. | So far, we've migrated every lesson planned for this week. |

The common response **so far, so good** means **hasta ahora, todo bien**.

In a distance context, **so far** can instead mean **tan lejos**: **I didn't expect us to walk so far → No esperaba que camináramos tan lejos**.

## **Or So** Meaning Approximately

After a number or amount, **or so** means **aproximadamente**, **más o menos** or **unos/unas**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| La reparación tardará unas dos semanas. | The repair will take two weeks or so. |

Use **or something like that** for an approximate description: **It was a warning or something like that → Era una advertencia o algo así**. The two expressions overlap only when approximation is involved.

## **And So On**

**And so on** continues an obvious list and means **etcétera**, **y así sucesivamente** or **y demás**.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El formulario pide el nombre, la dirección, el teléfono y demás. | The form asks for your name, address, phone number, and so on. |

Use it only when the listener can reasonably infer the omitted items.

## **So-Called**

**So-called** introduces the name people give someone or something. It can be neutral, but it often signals doubt or criticism.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| El supuesto experto no pudo responder ninguna pregunta básica. | The so-called expert couldn't answer a single basic question. |

The skeptical context makes **so-called** equivalent to **supuesto** here.

## **So-So**

Informal **so-so** means neither good nor bad.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| —¿Cómo estuvo la comida? —Regular; el plato principal estaba frío. | “How was the food?” “So-so; the main course was cold.” |

## Homophones: **So, Sew** and **Sow**

**So**, **sew** and the verb **sow** are pronounced `/soʊ/`.

- **so** performs the functions taught in this lesson;
- **sew** means **coser**;
- **sow** means **sembrar**.

The noun **sow**, meaning an adult female pig, is a heteronym pronounced `/saʊ/`.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Coso tantas bolsas y siembro tantas semillas que termino muy cansada. | I sew so many bags and sow so many seeds that I end up so tired. |

## Three Mastery Sentences

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Entonces, coser es tan fácil que tantas personas cosen tanto, y yo también lo creo. | So, sewing is so easy that so many people sew so much, and I think so too. |
| Laura tiene tanto trabajo, así que se quedará unas dos horas más; si es así, yo también me quedaré para que no trabaje sola. | Laura's got so much work, so she'll stay for two hours or so; if so, I'll stay too so that she won't work alone. |
| —El supuesto experto dice que el plan está regular. —Yo también lo creo. —Yo también, pero ¿y qué? Hasta ahora hemos arreglado el presupuesto, el cronograma y demás. | “The so-called expert says the plan is so-so.” “I think so too.” “So do I, but so what? So far, we've fixed the budget, the schedule, and so on.” |

Together, these sentences exercise conversational **so**, degree **so**, **so much**, **so many**, result **so**, purpose **so that**, agreement **so do I / too**, idea-replacing **so**, **if so**, **or so**, **so what**, **so far**, **so-called**, **so-so**, **and so on**, contractions and the homophones **so/sew/sow**.
