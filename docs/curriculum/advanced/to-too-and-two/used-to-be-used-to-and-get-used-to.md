# **Used To, Be Used To**, and **Get Used To**

> **I used to avoid walking, but I'm used to walking now because I got used to doing it every day.**

These three patterns look similar but express a past habit, a present state of familiarity, and the process of becoming familiar.

## **Used To + Base Verb**

Use **used to + base verb** for a past habit or state that is no longer true.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Antes solía conducir al trabajo, pero ahora voy caminando. | I used to drive to work, but now I walk. |

The contrast with **now** confirms that the old habit changed.

Questions and negatives normally use **did + use to**:

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿Antes trabajabas los sábados? | Did you use to work on Saturdays? |
| Antes no tomaba café. | I didn't use to drink coffee. |

After **did/didn't**, write **use to** because **did** already carries the past.

Do not confuse **used to** with **usually**:

- **I used to work here**: I worked here in the past but no longer do.
- **I usually work here**: this is my normal present habit.

## **Be Used To + Noun or -ing**

Use **be used to** for something that is already familiar or normal.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Estoy acostumbrada al ruido porque estoy acostumbrada a trabajar cerca del aeropuerto. | I'm used to the noise because I'm used to working near the airport. |

Here **to** is a preposition, so the PING rule gives **to working**, not *to work*.

The form of **be** changes with the time and person:

- **I'm used to it.**
- **She was used to it.**
- **They'll be used to it soon.**

## **Get Used To + Noun or -ing**

Use **get used to** for the process of becoming familiar.

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Al principio el ruido me molestaba, pero me acostumbré a trabajar cerca del aeropuerto. | At first the noise bothered me, but I got used to working near the airport. |

This **to** is also a preposition, so use **working**.

The form of **get** shows the time:

- **I'm getting used to it**: the process is happening now.
- **I got used to it**: the process was completed.
- **I'll get used to it**: the adjustment is expected in the future.

## The Three-Way Contrast

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Antes trabajaba de noche. | I used to work at night. |
| Estoy acostumbrada a trabajar de noche. | I'm used to working at night. |
| Me estoy acostumbrando a trabajar de noche. | I'm getting used to working at night. |

The direct bridge is:

- **solía hacer → used to do**
- **estar acostumbrado a hacer → be used to doing**
- **acostumbrarse a hacer → get used to doing**

## Pronunciation

In affirmative **used to**, the final sounds merge, and the phrase commonly sounds close to `/ˈjuːstə/`. A separate **-d** is not clearly heard.

In **be/get used to**, the sequence can sound very similar in connected speech: **I'm used to it** may also contain `/juːstə/`. Use the structure and meaning to distinguish them:

- **used to + base verb**: past habit;
- **be/get used to + noun or -ing**: familiarity or adjustment.

## Three Mastery Sentences

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Antes no caminaba al trabajo, pero me acostumbré a hacerlo y ahora estoy acostumbrada al trayecto. | I didn't use to walk to work, but I got used to doing it, and now I'm used to the journey. |
| ¿Antes trabajabas de noche o todavía te estás acostumbrando a trabajar en este horario? | Did you use to work at night, or are you still getting used to working this schedule? |
| Ella suele conducir, antes conducía de noche y está acostumbrada a conducir en tráfico pesado. | She usually drives, she used to drive at night, and she's used to driving in heavy traffic. |

Together, these sentences contrast **usually**, past **used to**, familiar **be used to**, changing **get used to**, negative and question forms, and prepositional **to + -ing**.
