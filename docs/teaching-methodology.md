# Teaching Methodology

This is the working reference for the teaching principles behind Inglés Con Confianza.

When the owner explains a teaching principle, lesson behavior, or preferred student experience, add it here. Use this document when designing curriculum data, lesson steps, prompts, feedback, and learner-facing UI.

## Audience and language

- The initial learner is a Spanish-speaking adult, with Latin American Spanish as the default context.
- Learner-facing explanations and UI should be in clear, natural Latin American Spanish.
- Use the `tú` form when speaking directly to one learner.
- English remains the target language being produced, so English answers, examples, and target forms should remain visible where they are being taught.
- Do not use grammatical terminology with the student unless it is absolutely necessary for the task. Terms such as “present participle” belong in teacher and course-design documentation, not in the learner-facing lesson.

## Teacher voice

- The teacher explains briefly, then invites the learner to discover or retrieve the answer.
- The tone should feel warm, energetic, encouraging, and immediately useful.
- Lessons should create frequent moments of: “¡Ah, es así de fácil!”
- Use short explanations, visible patterns, quick questions, and immediate positive feedback.
- The learner is an adult. The experience can be playful and visually engaging without becoming childish.
- Emojis and symbols such as `✨`, `🎉`, and `😊` are welcome when they reinforce encouragement or a milestone. Use them intentionally rather than decorating every sentence.

## Live-teaching lesson loop

The core lesson rhythm is:

1. Explain a small idea.
2. Show a clear example or visual pattern.
3. Ask the learner to retrieve the answer.
4. Accept the answer immediately when it is correct.
5. Give short, positive feedback.
6. Build the idea into a larger phrase or sentence.
7. Reuse the idea in a later question.

The application should feel like a patient teacher guiding a live conversation, not like a worksheet full of instructions.

## Flow, challenge, and momentum

One major inspiration is Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's concept of **flow**: the learner should stay in the sweet spot where the task is not so easy that it becomes boring and not so difficult that it becomes frustrating.

- Lessons should create constant small wins.
- Progress should feel incremental, visible, and earned.
- Each step should ask for one reachable action, then quickly move forward.
- If a learner is getting everything instantly, increase challenge by combining known pieces into larger phrases.
- If a learner stalls, reduce difficulty with hints, smaller blocks, clearer contrast, or a near-identical easier question.
- Avoid long stretches of passive reading before the learner gets to act.
- Do not punish mistakes; use them as signals that the next step needs a smaller bridge.
- The ideal Practice rhythm is rapid but not frantic: glance, understand, answer, win, continue.

This matters especially because the learner experience should compete with modern attention patterns. Practice should feel focused and paced like a short-form interaction without becoming shallow: no distractions, no admin chrome, one clear action, immediate response, and visible movement through the lesson.

## First-attempt answerability

Every assessed prompt must give the learner a fair opportunity to answer correctly on the first attempt. A lesson may challenge recall or ask the learner to combine known pieces, but it must not require an untaught translation, an unexplained spelling change, or a grammatical leap.

- Before asking for a word or phrase, teach its mapping directly or demonstrate a pattern that makes the answer genuinely predictable.
- Treat inference as fair only when the lesson has shown the relevant pattern clearly and the target follows it without a hidden exception.
- Build cumulative sentences exclusively from language already introduced and retrieved. Audit every answer block, including connectors, pronouns, punctuation, capitalization, and phrase-specific prepositions.
- When a natural English phrase cannot be derived word by word, teach and assess the complete phrase. For example, teach `importante para mí` as `important to me` when expressing personal significance instead of composing the misleading literal answer `important for me`.
- A learner may still forget or mistype an answer. That is different from the course asking for knowledge it never supplied.
- If a learner cannot reasonably succeed on the first attempt, the problem is in the lesson sequence: add a bridge, regroup the prompt, or remove the premature target.

First-attempt answerability does not mean making every task trivial. Difficulty should come from retrieving and recombining taught language, not from guessing what the course author had in mind.

## One teaching focus at a time

Learners are not language-processing systems that can absorb several new rules simultaneously. Even when every answer is technically present in an explanation, a dense screen can force the learner to divide attention across patterns, exceptions, spelling changes, and sentence structure. That is not a fair learning task.

- Give each explanation one primary teaching focus.
- Do not introduce two productive patterns and their exceptions in the same explanation.
- Keep explanation text short enough that the learner can hold its single idea in working memory while answering the next question.
- Follow every small piece of information with immediate, focused application before presenting the next piece.
- Teach exceptions one at a time after the regular pattern is stable. Explain the exact difference, then retrieve it immediately.
- Begin a practice table with an example the learner has just seen. Continue with a small number of items that use the same pattern and no unexplained exceptions.
- Move into sentence building only after each required word, pattern, or exception has completed its own explanation-and-retrieval cycle.
- A cumulative sentence may combine several known pieces, but it should introduce no new teaching focus of its own.

A useful authoring rhythm is: show one or two examples, immediately confirm those same examples, briefly explain the pattern they reveal, immediately apply it to a few new examples, explain one exception, practice that exception, and then reuse the established language in a sentence.

## Micro-steps and pace

Chunking information into many brief interactions is part of the teaching method, not merely a UI preference.

- Prefer several fast, focused screens over one screen containing the same total information.
- Alternate information and action: a little information, immediate application, another little piece, immediate application.
- Do not make the learner hold an explanation in working memory for a long time before using it.
- Let each screen do one job: show, confirm, explain, predict, or combine.
- Keep the pace energetic by creating frequent transitions and wins, while giving the learner control over advancing.
- Treat a presentation with ten short, single-purpose slides as a better model than one dense slide shown for the same total amount of time.

This rapid cadence is intentionally similar to the appeal of short-form media: attention is continually renewed. The educational substance remains deep because each quick interaction contributes to a carefully sequenced concept rather than becoming disconnected entertainment.

## Short lessons and the “one more” effect

Lesson boundaries should be designed for human motivation, not mathematical neatness. Material that forms one logical concept may still work better as several very short lessons.

- Prefer several lessons of roughly two minutes over one lesson that asks for a much larger continuous commitment.
- Make the next lesson look short and approachable before the learner starts it.
- End lessons at natural micro-milestones so each completion feels meaningful rather than arbitrary.
- Use a small completion to create momentum toward the next lesson: the desired learner reaction is, “That was quick—I can do one more.”
- Do not keep adding steps merely because they belong to the same linguistic family. Split when the number of interactions makes the lesson feel substantial or tiring.
- Preserve continuity across the split: the next short lesson should immediately reuse the previous lesson's final idea so separate lessons still feel like one guided progression.
- Give each short lesson a clear, achievable promise. Its title and opening should make the learner understand what they will be able to produce within the next few minutes.

Five short lessons may be easier to begin and finish than one lesson containing the same total work. This is not a contradiction: perceived commitment, frequent completion, and renewed attention materially change the learner experience.

## Confidence-building lesson endings

Every lesson should end by showing the learner that its small pieces produce useful real-life English.

- Finish with a challenge question or a complete sentence that is common in everyday speech.
- Make the final task feel like a meaningful achievement, not merely the last row in a vocabulary list.
- Build the ending entirely from language taught and retrieved before that point. A confidence question must remain first-attempt answerable.
- Let the final task combine known pieces and feel more substantial than the preceding micro-steps without introducing a new concept.
- Prefer language the learner can imagine saying soon: a useful statement, request, question, response, or personal expression.
- For a pattern-focused lesson, introduce only the minimum additional high-utility language needed to turn the pattern into a real sentence, and teach that language before the final challenge.
- Use the ending to answer the learner's implicit question: “What can I say now that I could not say a few minutes ago?”

The emotional purpose is confidence through evidence. The learner should finish with proof that the lesson applies outside the exercise and carry that success into the next short lesson.

## Incidental vocabulary and context hints

A sentence may occasionally need a specific non-core word to create useful or memorable context. For example, a lesson about `quiero comprar` might use `Quiero comprar el arete` even when `arete` is not itself a teaching target.

- Prefer sentences made entirely from known, high-utility language.
- Use an answer-revealing context hint only when the unfamiliar item is incidental, unusually specific, and not worth interrupting the lesson to teach formally; for example, `arete = earring`.
- Keep the hinted word outside the skill being assessed. The learner should still perform the meaningful target operation, such as producing `I want to buy` from previously taught pieces.
- Do not use context hints to smuggle in core vocabulary, connectors, structural words, spelling exceptions, or the lesson's primary concept. Teach those before testing them.
- Avoid stacking several supplied words into one sentence. If hints are doing most of the translation work, simplify the sentence or teach the missing language first.
- Distinguish persistent authored context hints from learner-requested help. A context hint supplies deliberately incidental information; the help control supports retrieval of an answer the learner was already equipped to know.

Context hints are a limited tool for preserving a good sentence, not a substitute for curriculum sequencing.

## The student is never wrong

The learner may give an answer that does not yet match the target, but the student is never treated as wrong. An unsuccessful attempt is useful evidence about what bridge, hint, or repetition they need next—not a failure and never a source of shame.

- Make every attempt feel safe. Do not scold, subtract points, play a failure sound, or use language and visuals that embarrass the learner.
- Let the learner request help as many times as needed. Hints and revealed answers carry no penalty.
- Treat using help as continued participation and forward movement, not as cheating or a lesser kind of success.
- When an answer does not match, keep the response neutral and useful: preserve the learner's momentum, offer a smaller bridge, or let them try again immediately.
- Design each interaction so it can end in a win. If retrieval does not happen today, repeated supported exposure can make it happen later.
- Future learner analytics may use attempts and help requests to choose better review, but never to shame the learner or portray them as incapable.

The product should communicate patient confidence: **you are not failing; you are still learning, and we will stay with you until this becomes familiar.**

## Discovery over memorization

- Prefer reusable patterns over isolated vocabulary lists.
- Teach patterns from the bottom up: show a few clear examples first, then help the learner name the rule those examples reveal.
- Do not normally begin with an abstract rule and ask the learner to apply it top down. Most learners recognize a pattern across concrete examples more easily than they operationalize a verbal principle.
- Put each comparison on its own line so the learner can see and compare the changing pieces without extracting them from a paragraph.
- Show enough examples for the learner to infer the pattern, but keep them focused on one change at a time.
- Bold or highlight the part that changes:

  `visit`**`ando`** ⟶ `visit`**`ing`**

- Keep the explanation close to the examples it explains.
- Avoid burying the important pattern in a paragraph of grammar terminology.
- Name the useful pattern directly instead of naming the grammatical category behind it.

The preferred sequence is:

1. Invite attention with a short cue such as `Mira esto`.
2. Show one concrete source-and-target pair.
3. Show a second pair with the same visible relationship.
4. Immediately ask the learner to confirm the examples they just saw.
5. State the small rule the examples reveal in learner-friendly language.
6. Briefly communicate the rule's usefulness or reach.
7. Invite prediction with a short prompt such as `Adivina los siguientes`.
8. Ask the learner to apply the pattern to a few new examples.

The rule confirms what the learner has begun to notice; it is not the first thing they must decipher.

## Questions and answers

- Questions should be short and written in Spanish.
- Highlight the exact Spanish word or phrase the learner needs to translate.
- The learner should be able to type the answer directly.
- Correct answers should be recognized while typing, visibly marked, and move the lesson forward automatically.
- There is no penalty for an incorrect answer.
- A learner can request help as often as needed with a visible help control or the `Alt + H` shortcut, without penalty.
- Help may briefly reveal the answer, then fade away so the learner can try again.
- Accept natural alternatives when the lesson is teaching that variability.
- Contractions are important. For example, accept both `I want to go` and `I wanna go` when the lesson is teaching that conversational contrast.
- Phrase-level answers are appropriate when translating individual blocks would produce a misleading result, such as `para comprar` ⟶ `to buy`.

## Pronunciation bridges

North American pronunciation is the initial spoken-English reference for the course. Whenever English spelling would lead a Spanish speaker to a noticeably different or unintelligible pronunciation, teach the pronunciation explicitly when the word or phrase is introduced.

- Do not assume the learner can derive an irregular pronunciation from English spelling.
- Call out silent or reduced sounds, unexpected consonant sounds, surprising vowel sounds, stress, and compressed syllables when they materially affect understandable speech.
- Use a simple learner-facing respelling made from familiar letters, such as `different` → `DIFF-rent`.
- Capitalize the stressed syllable and state the syllable count when that makes the rhythm clearer: `DIFF-rent, dos sílabas`.
- Repeat the pronunciation in a short standalone form after explaining it so the learner can rehearse the whole sound.
- Always keep the correctly spelled English word visible. The pronunciation bridge supports speech; it is not an alternative spelling.
- Add pronunciation notes only when the spelling is genuinely misleading for the target learner. Do not clutter every transparent word with another notation.
- Future voice-overs should align with the same pronunciation bridge and model a natural North American form. When useful, audio may present a clear segmented version followed by the natural full-speed version.

Do not use the International Phonetic Alphabet in learner-facing lessons. Requiring a Spanish-speaking learner to master a third symbol system in order to pronounce a second language adds unnecessary cognitive load. IPA may remain an internal linguistic reference if needed, but it is not part of the teaching interface or expected learner knowledge.

## Building language from blocks

- Keep reusable curriculum objects atomic when they represent useful language pieces.
- Allow lesson questions to group blocks when the meaning or English translation depends on the phrase as a whole.
- Do not force a one-Spanish-block-to-one-English-word relationship when that teaches the wrong structure.
- Build from small mappings toward complete, meaningful sentences.

## UI principles

- Keep one clear teaching action visible at a time.
- Avoid repeating the same instruction on every question card.
- Use progress indicators to make the learner feel movement.
- Support keyboard-first use, with visible shortcut hints where helpful.
- Allow the learner to move backward and forward between lesson segments.
- Use strong visual hierarchy: large target phrases, bold patterns, clear input fields, and short feedback.
- Prefer a little visual energy over a cold academic interface, while keeping the screen uncluttered.

## Current implementation boundary

- Text interaction comes first.
- Voice input, speech recognition, pronunciation scoring, and audio feedback can come later.
- The first lesson implementation should test the teaching rhythm before we design a generalized lesson authoring system.
- Reusable curriculum data and lesson sequencing are related but separate concerns: curriculum objects describe language; lesson steps describe how the teacher introduces and practices it.
